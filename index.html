Revised Research Essay

The Effects of AI on the Success of Learning in Mechanical Engineering Students
Brody Golightly · ENC 2135 · Florida State University

About this revision: This version keeps the original essay's central claim and research base while strengthening the organization, transitions, and focus. The discussion moves from AI in education generally to mechanical engineering applications, student concerns, and classroom guidelines. It also makes the limitation of the retracted Xue and Wang source explicit rather than presenting it as strong evidence.

Introduction

Over the last several years, artificial intelligence (AI)—the simulation of human intelligence in machines—has become a more common part of everyday life. One major development is generative artificial intelligence (GAI), which can create written responses, explanations, images, code, and other content from a user's prompt. These tools have also become controversial in education. Students can use GAI to complete assignments instead of working through problems themselves, which can lead to academic dishonesty and interfere with the development of important skills. However, AI is not automatically harmful to learning. A student might use it to complete an assignment, but that same student could also ask it to explain an unfamiliar concept, provide another example, or create practice questions. The important issue is how the technology is used.

This issue is especially important in mechanical engineering. Students must understand challenging topics in mathematics, physics, mechanics, thermodynamics, and control systems while developing strong problem-solving skills. AI could give students another resource when they struggle with a concept, but it can also make it easier to avoid doing the work required to learn it. Research on mechanical engineering education points to both possibilities: students recognize the potential for more interactive learning, while researchers also identify limitations and ethical concerns (Akhavan-Safar et al., 2026). AI should be used more within mechanical engineering classes as a tool that helps students learn, provided that instructors establish clear expectations, students verify AI-generated information, and the technology supports rather than replaces their own reasoning.

AI as an additional learning resource

Before considering mechanical engineering specifically, it is useful to consider how AI may support students more generally. One potential benefit is that it can offer another explanation when a student does not understand information presented in class. Xue and Wang (2022) discuss the use of AI to support teaching and provide practice and feedback based on students' learning needs. Their article has since been retracted, however, so it should not be treated as strong evidence for this argument. The general possibility of individualized explanations is relevant, but the main case for using AI in mechanical engineering should rest on stronger and more directly related research.

The value of additional explanations becomes clearer in mechanical engineering, where students often need to understand both a mathematical equation and the physical meaning behind it. A student might memorize an equation without understanding what it represents in an actual engineering situation. An AI tool could offer a different explanation, walk through a simpler example, or connect an equation to a physical system. Used this way, it gives the student another route toward understanding rather than simply supplying a final answer.

AI in mechanical engineering problem-solving

Impelluso (2026) describes AI as a tool that can guide students through difficult problem-solving rather than simply provide the final answer. He characterizes it as a “navigator of structured solution spaces” instead of a black-box solver. This distinction matters because students may become dependent on AI if they use it to handle every difficult part of an assignment. Completing more problems does not necessarily mean that a student is becoming better at engineering. If students use AI to identify a step they do not understand and then work through the problem themselves, the technology can become part of the learning process.

This kind of support may be particularly useful in courses with abstract concepts. Hu et al. (2026) studied the use of GAI in a Fundamentals of Mechanical Engineering Control course. They describe the difficulty of control topics as connected to their “highly abstract mathematical nature and disconnect from physical intuition.” Their study discusses using GAI for text generation, visualization, and code generation to make abstract theories more interactive. For example, a student could ask for an explanation of how changing a variable affects a control system, request an example of a system that demonstrates a concept, or explore code that visualizes a result. The student still needs to understand what the model or code is doing, but these activities can help connect mathematical ideas to engineering situations.

Student perspectives and responsible use

The way students view AI is another important part of its role in education. Students decide whether they use these tools to learn or as a shortcut. Akhavan-Safar et al. (2026) studied mechanical engineering students' perspectives on video learning and AI-based tools. Their findings indicate that students see potential for more interactive learning experiences, while also recognizing limitations and ethical concerns. Access to AI by itself does not guarantee that students will use it productively.

Consider a student who is struggling with a free-body diagram. The student could ask AI to solve the entire assigned problem and copy the result without learning how to identify or analyze the forces. Alternatively, the student could ask what a free-body diagram represents, request an explanation of which forces are commonly considered, and then attempt the problem independently. The second approach still requires the student to do the engineering work. The difference illustrates why the way AI is used matters more than access alone.

There are legitimate concerns about misuse and over-reliance. Alghazo et al. (2025) examined GAI in mechanical engineering education and identified ethical misuse and dependence on AI as challenges that may weaken critical thinking. Their study also emphasizes training, clear policies, and gradual implementation. One student described AI as useful in projects but not in assignments because of ethical concerns and the possible loss of critical thinking (Alghazo et al., 2025). This concern captures the central tension: the same tool that can help explain difficult material can also allow students to avoid the thinking that education is meant to develop.

Accuracy is another concern. AI-generated answers can sound convincing while containing incorrect assumptions, calculations, or explanations. Frenkel and Emara (2024) tested ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 on undergraduate mechanical engineering exams and the Fundamentals of Engineering mechanical engineering exam. They reported that GPT-4 answered 76% of the questions correctly, compared with 51% for GPT-3.5. These results show that AI may be able to answer many engineering questions, but students should not assume every response is correct. They should compare answers with textbooks, lecture notes, equations, calculations, and other reliable sources. They should also be able to explain why a result makes sense.

How instructors can integrate AI

Instructors can create opportunities for students to use AI while still requiring them to demonstrate their own understanding. For example, a professor could allow AI-generated hints during selected practice assignments but require students to submit their calculations and explain their reasoning. Another activity could ask students to examine an AI-generated solution and identify errors. Because AI can make mistakes, analyzing those mistakes could become a way to practice checking calculations and applying engineering principles.

Clear course policies are important because students should not have to guess which uses are acceptable. Alghazo et al. (2025) discuss training, policies, and gradual implementation as parts of introducing GAI into mechanical engineering education. Instructors could explain when AI may be used, how students should verify information, and which tasks must be completed independently. A practical set of guidelines could allow AI for concept explanations, practice questions, additional examples, and feedback on a student's reasoning. It could prohibit using AI to complete work that is intended to measure the student's own problem-solving ability. Students should also be prepared to explain the work they submit without relying on the AI to explain it for them.

This approach follows Impelluso's (2026) idea of AI as a guide rather than a replacement for student thinking. In mechanical engineering, producing a numerical answer is not enough; students need to understand why a solution works and whether it is physically reasonable. Learning to evaluate AI output may also help prepare students for workplaces where AI tools are used in design, programming, simulation, and analysis. Avoiding the technology entirely may leave students less prepared to assess it, but using it without developing fundamental engineering knowledge would create a different problem.

Conclusion

AI should not be viewed only as a problem in mechanical engineering education. It can give students additional explanations, practice opportunities, and ways to explore difficult concepts. At the same time, research identifies concerns about academic dishonesty, over-reliance, and inaccurate information. These risks make it important to teach students how to use AI carefully rather than assume that access will automatically improve learning.

The most useful role for AI in mechanical engineering courses is as a tutor, guide, or practice resource—not an answer machine. Students can ask questions, request different explanations, create practice problems, and check their reasoning while remaining responsible for understanding the material. Professors can support this approach through clear policies and assignments that require students to show their own work. The goal should not be to make AI do engineering for students. It should be to use AI in ways that help students become better engineers themselves.

04 / Reflective process letter

Letter to My Professor

Reflecting on genres, composing, and rhetorical strategies · Approximately 1281 words

Dear Professor Cardelli,

Throughout this course, I have learned that writing is more than putting information into paragraphs and correcting grammar. The projects asked me to think about why I was writing, who I was writing to, and what form would communicate my ideas most effectively. Before this course, I often approached writing as a process of explaining a topic and supporting it with information. I now understand that the way a message is presented can be just as important as the information itself. Purpose, audience, genre, evidence, and revision all influence how a reader understands a piece of writing.

My research project focused on the effects of artificial intelligence on learning in mechanical engineering students. I chose this topic because AI is becoming increasingly common in education, but there is disagreement about whether it helps students learn or encourages them to avoid doing their own work. I wanted to move beyond treating AI as either completely good or completely bad. My main argument became that AI should be used more in mechanical engineering classes as a learning tool, as long as it is integrated responsibly. Students should be able to use it for explanations, practice, and feedback, but they should still develop and demonstrate their own problem-solving skills.

The research essay outline helped me organize the argument from a broad issue to a more specific one. I planned to introduce AI in education generally, then discuss how it could affect mechanical engineering, and finally consider student perspectives and responsible classroom use. This order gave readers background before asking them to consider the specific needs of engineering students. It also helped me see that an outline is not just a list of topics. It is a plan for how readers will encounter an argument and how each section will contribute to the thesis.

Research also taught me to pay attention to what a source can and cannot support. The essay uses research about mechanical engineering students' perspectives, AI implementation, control systems instruction, and AI performance on engineering exams. These sources helped me discuss both potential benefits and limitations. I also identified that the Xue and Wang article had been retracted. That meant I could not treat it as strong evidence simply because its ideas supported my point. Recognizing that limitation was an important part of making the essay more responsible. I learned that research is not just collecting quotations that agree with me; it requires evaluating relevance, reliability, and the strength of the evidence.

One of the most important ideas in the essay was the difference between using AI as a tutor and using it as an answer machine. Impelluso's description of AI as a “navigator of structured solution spaces” gave me a way to explain the role I thought AI should have. Hu and colleagues' work on abstract control-system concepts helped me make the argument more specific to mechanical engineering. I could explain that students sometimes understand mathematical steps without seeing how those steps connect to a physical system. AI-generated explanations, visualizations, or examples may help bridge that gap, provided students still evaluate what the tool produces.

The essay also required me to address concerns rather than ignore them. Alghazo and colleagues discuss ethical misuse and over-reliance, while Frenkel and Emara's testing shows that AI answers are not always correct. Including these concerns made the argument more balanced. It also gave me a stronger reason for recommending clear course policies. If instructors explain which uses are allowed and require students to show their reasoning, students have less uncertainty about expectations. They can use AI for learning without confusing assistance with having the tool complete the assignment.

Another major lesson from the course was how much audience affects genre. Mechanical engineering students and professors may care about the same issue, but they need different kinds of information. Students may want quick, practical examples they can apply while studying. Instructors may want a more developed argument that recognizes academic integrity concerns and proposes workable classroom guidelines. A single essay cannot always serve both audiences equally well. This helped me understand why a writer might adapt the same central message into an infographic, a social media post, or an open letter.

Each genre creates different expectations. An infographic needs a clear visual hierarchy and short pieces of information that readers can understand quickly. A social media post needs to be concise and engaging, with a message that is easy to remember. An open letter gives the writer more room to explain a position, acknowledge opposing views, and make a request of a specific audience. I learned that changing genres is not simply changing the appearance of a document. It means reconsidering the purpose, organization, tone, level of detail, and kinds of evidence that will work for the intended readers.

Rhetorical strategies also became more practical to me through these projects. Logos mattered because my argument depended on research findings and examples. Ethos mattered because I needed to show that I understood instructors' concerns and was not dismissing academic integrity. Acknowledging the limitations of AI and the retracted source helped make the discussion more credible. Pathos was not the main strategy, but it was relevant when considering how frustrating it can be for students to struggle with difficult technical concepts and need another way to understand them. I learned that rhetorical appeals work together; they are not just labels to add to an essay.

The composing process was not completely linear. My ideas became clearer as I moved between outlining, researching, drafting, and considering the audience. At times, I needed to add a specific engineering example because a point was too general. At other times, I needed to shorten or reorganize information so the main claim would be easier to follow. Revision therefore involved more than fixing sentences. It meant checking whether the structure supported the thesis, whether each paragraph had a purpose, whether the evidence was being used accurately, and whether the tone fit the reader.

Creating this portfolio is another part of that process because it asks me to select work and explain what the work demonstrates. The research essay shows how I developed and supported an argument. The outline shows planning and organization before drafting. The responsible-use checklist translates the argument into practical advice for students. Together, these pieces show that writing can change form while keeping a consistent purpose. They also show that composing includes planning, selecting evidence, adapting to readers, and reflecting on what could be improved.

I expect these skills to matter beyond this English course. Mechanical engineers have to explain technical decisions, communicate with people who have different backgrounds, and make complex information understandable. A technically correct answer is not always enough if the person receiving it cannot understand the reasoning or why the result matters. Being able to adjust a message for a particular audience is therefore part of effective engineering communication. I have learned to think more deliberately about how I present information and how I can make an argument clear without oversimplifying it.

Overall, this course has helped me see writing as a series of choices rather than one fixed formula. I can use research to build credibility, examples to clarify technical ideas, and genre conventions to shape how readers encounter an argument. I have also learned that a strong draft is not necessarily finished after the first attempt. It improves when I reconsider its purpose, structure, evidence, and audience. I will carry these lessons into future academic and professional work, especially when I need to communicate complicated engineering ideas clearly and responsibly.

Thank you for the opportunity to work through these projects and develop a better understanding of writing and communication.

Sincerely,
Brody Golightly
